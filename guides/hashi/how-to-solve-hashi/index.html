How to Solve Hashi (Bridges) Puzzles: Rules and Tips for Beginners

Updated 1 November 2026

Hashi, also called Bridges or Hashiwokakero, is a logic puzzle played with numbered circles called islands. Your goal is to connect the islands with straight bridges, so that each island has exactly as many bridges as its number and all the islands join up into one group. You never need to guess: every bridge can be worked out from the numbers.

In short. Start with islands that have only one or two neighbours, and with big numbers that leave no room for choice. Every bridge you draw uses up part of two islands' numbers, which narrows down the next move.

What is Hashi?

Hashi comes from Japan, where its full name, Hashiwokakero, means "build bridges". Each island shows a number from 1 to 8, and you draw bridges between islands in straight lines across or down the grid.

You only need to count to eight. The challenge is fitting all the bridges in without crossings and without leaving any island cut off.

Hashi rules

  1. Bridges run straight across or down, between two islands, with no islands in between.
  2. Two islands can be joined by one or two bridges, never more.
  3. Bridges may not cross each other.
  4. The number on an island is how many bridges end there.
  5. All the islands must end up connected in one group.

Three Hashi solving techniques

1. Islands with only one neighbour

If an island can only reach one other island, all of its bridges go there. A 2 with one neighbour gets a double bridge, and a 1 gets a single one.

2. Big numbers with few neighbours

Each neighbour can take at most two bridges. A 4 in a corner has only two neighbours, so it needs a double bridge to both. Likewise, a 3 with two neighbours needs at least one bridge to each.

3. Count what each island still needs

After drawing a bridge, subtract it from both islands. An island that has already reached its number is finished, and an island with only one way left to get its remaining bridges must use it. Keep an eye on the whole picture too: a pair of islands that would finish each other off and form a closed group is not allowed while other islands remain.

Worked example: solving a small Hashi step by step

Follow along with a pencil. Bridges shown in orange are the ones we have just drawn. Rows are numbered 1 to 4 from the top, and columns 1 to 5 from the left.

  1. Here is the puzzle: six islands. Each needs as many bridges as its number.

  2. One neighbour. The 2 in the top-right corner can only reach the 3 to its left. It needs both its bridges there: a double bridge.

  3. One neighbour again. The 1 in row 2 has nothing beside it and nothing above it. Its only neighbour is the 2 below, so the bridge goes there.

  4. Count what's left. The 3 at the top already has two bridges. It needs one more, and the only island left to reach is the 2 on its left.

  5. Count again. The top-left 2 has one bridge and needs one more. Its only other neighbour is the 2 below it.

  6. Finish. The bottom-left 2 needs one more bridge, and so does the bottom-right 2. Join them, and every island has its number. All six islands are connected in one group.

Common Hashi mistakes

  • Crossing bridges. Before drawing a long bridge, check that no bridge already runs across its path.
  • Forgetting the limit of two. Two islands can share at most a double bridge, never three.
  • Leaving an island group cut off. Everything must connect in the end. Two islands that finish each other off too early can't join the rest.
  • Guessing. If you're stuck, find the island with the fewest neighbours compared to its number.

Hashi questions and answers

Are Hashi, Bridges and Hashiwokakero the same puzzle?

Yes. Hashiwokakero is the full Japanese name, Hashi is short for it, and Bridges is the English name. The rules are identical.

Can bridges bend around corners?

No. Every bridge is a straight line across or down, between two islands.

What size should a beginner start with?

A small grid with up to about ten islands is perfect for learning. Larger grids have more islands but use the same ideas.

Does every Hashi have only one solution?

A well-made puzzle does. The puzzles in this guide were checked by computer to confirm there is exactly one solution.
