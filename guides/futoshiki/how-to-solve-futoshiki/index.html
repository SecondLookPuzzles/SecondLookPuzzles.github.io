How to Solve Futoshiki Puzzles: Rules and Tips for Beginners

Updated 1 November 2026

Futoshiki, also called More or Less, is a logic puzzle played on a small square grid. Your goal is to fill every square so that each row and each column contains every number once, while obeying the greater-than and less-than signs between squares. You never need to guess: every square can be worked out from the numbers and signs.

In short. The signs do most of the work. Use them together with the numbers still missing from a row or column, follow chains of signs, and remember that the smallest number can never sit on the big side of a sign.

What is Futoshiki?

Futoshiki comes from Japan, and its name means "inequality". Puzzles range from 4×4 grids for beginners up to 9×9. A few numbers are given, and small signs between squares say which neighbour is bigger.

You only ever compare numbers: is this one bigger or smaller than that one? There is no adding or multiplying.

Futoshiki rules

  1. In a grid of size 4, use the numbers 1 to 4; in a grid of size 5, use 1 to 5, and so on.
  2. Each row contains every number once.
  3. Each column contains every number once.
  4. A sign between two squares must be obeyed. It always points at the smaller number: in 2 < 4 the sign opens towards the 4. The signs ∧ and ∨ work the same way between rows.

Three Futoshiki solving techniques

1. Combine the signs with the missing numbers

When a row or column has only two or three numbers left to place, a single sign often decides where they go.

2. Follow chains of signs

Several signs in a row form a chain. In a chain of three increasing squares, the first square can't hold either of the two largest numbers, and the last can't hold either of the two smallest. Short chains often pin down every number.

3. Keep the smallest and largest numbers in mind

A square on the big side of a sign can never hold 1, and a square on the small side can never hold the largest number. When you are hunting for where the 1 or the largest number goes in a row, these squares are ruled out straight away.

Worked example: solving a 4×4 Futoshiki step by step

Follow along with a pencil. Numbers shown in orange are the ones we have just filled in. Rows are numbered 1 to 4 from the top, and columns 1 to 4 from the left.

  1. Here is the puzzle. Every row and column needs 1, 2, 3 and 4 once. Two ∨ signs in the last column say each square there is bigger than the one below it.

  2. Follow a chain. Column 4 already has its 4 at the bottom, so the top three squares hold 1, 2 and 3, getting smaller as you go down: 3, 2, 1.

  3. Signs and missing numbers. Row 3 now reads 3, blank, blank, 1, so it needs 2 and 4. The < sign says the left one is smaller: 2, then 4.

  4. Signs again. Row 4 needs 1 and 3. Column 2 must be bigger than the 2 next to it, so it is 3, and column 1 is 1.

  5. Missing number. Column 2 now has 4, 2 and 3, so its empty square in row 2 is 1.

  6. Rows and columns. Column 1 needs 2 and 4 in rows 1 and 2. Row 1 already has a 4, so row 1 gets the 2 and row 2 gets the 4.

  7. Finish. Row 1 is missing a 1 and row 2 is missing a 3, both in column 3. The puzzle is solved, and every sign is obeyed.

Common Futoshiki mistakes

  • Reading a sign backwards. The sign always points at the smaller number, like a little arrowhead. When in doubt, read it aloud: "2 is less than 4".
  • Forgetting the vertical signs. The ∧ and ∨ signs between rows count just as much as the ones inside a row.
  • Checking signs but not columns. Every number still has to appear once in its column as well as its row.
  • Guessing. If you're stuck, look for the longest chain of signs and work out what its ends can be.

Futoshiki questions and answers

Is Futoshiki the same as More or Less?

Yes. More or Less is a common English name for the same puzzle, and the rules are identical.

Do I need to be good at maths?

No. You only compare numbers to see which is bigger. There is no adding or multiplying.

What size should a beginner start with?

A 4×4 grid is perfect for learning, and 5×5 is a gentle next step. Larger grids rely more on long chains of signs.

Does every Futoshiki have only one solution?

A well-made puzzle does. The puzzles in this guide were checked by computer to confirm there is exactly one solution.
