How to Solve Kakuro Puzzles: Rules and Tips for Beginners

Updated 1 November 2026

Kakuro is a logic puzzle that works like a crossword with numbers. Your goal is to fill every white square with a digit from 1 to 9, so that each run of white squares adds up to the sum printed beside it, with no digit repeated in a run. It is also known as Cross Sums. You never need to guess: every square can be worked out from the sums.

In short. Start with sums that have only one set of digits, like 3 in two squares (always 1 and 2). Where two sums cross, the square needs a digit both allow. When a sum has one square left, that square is the sum minus the others.

What is Kakuro?

Kakuro first appeared in American puzzle magazines as Cross Sums, and got its Japanese name later. The board has grey squares and white squares. A grey square next to a run of white squares holds that run's sum. The run can go across or down, and a grey square can hold one sum for each.

Kakuro needs a little arithmetic, but only small additions. Most of the work is knowing which digits can make a sum.

Kakuro rules

  1. Fill every white square with a digit from 1 to 9.
  2. A run is an unbroken line of white squares, across or down. Its digits add up to the sum in the grey square before it: the top number is for the run to its right, and the bottom number is for the run below it.
  3. A digit can't repeat within one run.

Three Kakuro solving techniques

1. Find the sums with only one set of digits

Very small and very large sums can only be made one way. In two squares, 3 is always 1 and 2, and 4 is always 1 and 3 (2 and 2 would repeat a digit). At the other end, 17 is always 8 and 9, and 24 in three squares is always 7, 8 and 9.

2. Where two sums cross, use a digit both allow

A square belongs to one run across and one run down. Its digit must fit both.

3. Fill the last square of a sum

When a sum has one square left, that square is the sum minus the digits already there.

Worked example: solving a 4×4 Kakuro step by step

Follow along with a pencil. We name each run by its sum and where it is.

  1. Here is the puzzle: 10 white squares, with sums across and down.

  2. One set, then crossing. The top 17 across has two squares, so it is 8 and 9. The 16 down on the left has two squares, so it is 7 and 9. Their shared square, top left, can only be 9.

  3. Last squares. The top 17 now needs 8 next to the 9, and the 16 down needs 7 below it.

  4. Crossing at the bottom right. The 4 down on the right is 1 and 3. The bottom 11 across shares a square with it, so its other square is 11 minus 1 or 11 minus 3: 10 or 8. That square is also in the 24 down (7, 8 and 9), so it is 8, and the square next to it is 3. The 4 down then needs 1 above the 3.

  5. The 22 across. It has 7 already, so its other two squares add up to 15. The first is in the 23 down, which is always 6, 8 and 9, and the 8 is used: it is 6 or 9. The second is in the 24 down, with the 8 used: 7 or 9. Only 6 and 9 make 15.

  6. Finish. The 23 down needs 23 minus 8 minus 6, which is 9. The 24 down needs 24 minus 9 minus 8, which is 7. The 17 across in the middle checks out: 9, 7 and 1.

Try a 5×5 practice puzzle

This puzzle has exactly one solution, and it can be solved with the three techniques above. Now and then a sum needs a digit that only one of its squares can take: that digit goes there.

Hint 1

Look for the sums with only one set: 16 in two squares is always 7 and 9 (there are three of them), and 29 in four squares is always 5, 7, 8 and 9.

Hint 2

The 29 across near the bottom crosses the 12 down on the right. Four different digits that add up to 12 can't include a 7, 8 or 9, so the crossing square is 5.

Common Kakuro mistakes

  • Repeating a digit in a run. 4 in two squares is 1 and 3, never 2 and 2.
  • Reading the wrong sum. In a grey square, the top number is for the run to the right, and the bottom number is for the run below.
  • Forgetting the crossing run. Every white square is in two runs. Check both before you write a digit.
  • Starting with the middle sums. Sums like 10 or 15 in two squares have many sets. Begin with the smallest and largest sums, which have one.
  • Guessing. A well-made puzzle always has a next step. Look for a crossing where only one digit fits.

Kakuro questions and answers

Is Kakuro the same as Cross Sums?

Yes. Cross Sums is the name it first had, and Kakuro is the name it got in Japan. The rules are the same.

Do I need to be good at maths?

Only a little. You add small numbers, never more than nine of them, and you soon learn the common sets by heart.

Which sums have only one set of digits?

In two squares: 3, 4, 16 and 17. In three squares: 6, 7, 23 and 24. In four squares: 10, 11, 29 and 30.

Does every Kakuro puzzle have only one solution?

A well-made puzzle does. The puzzles in this guide were checked by computer to confirm there is exactly one solution.
