How to Solve Binairo Puzzles: Rules and Tips for Beginners

Updated 1 November 2026

Binairo is a logic puzzle played on a square grid of black and white circles. Your goal is to fill every empty square so that no more than two circles of the same colour sit side by side, and every row and column has as many black circles as white ones. It is also known as Takuzu or the Binary Puzzle, and many people spell it Binario. You never need to guess: every square can be worked out from the rules.

In short. Three habits solve almost every beginner puzzle: look for pairs and gaps, count the black and white circles in each line, and check that no two rows or columns match.

What is Binairo?

Binairo (also called Takuzu or Binary Puzzle) uses only two symbols. Many puzzles print them as 0 and 1. This guide and our books use black and white circles, and the rules are the same either way. Puzzles come in several sizes, from small 4×4 grids that teach the rules to large grids for experts. Each puzzle starts with a few circles already in place. Those are fixed, and the rest is up to you.

No maths is needed. You are simply placing two colours so that a few rules hold.

Binairo rules

  1. Every square holds a black or a white circle.
  2. No more than two circles of the same colour may sit next to each other, in any row or column.
  3. Each row and each column has the same number of black and white circles. In a 6×6 grid, that means three of each.
  4. No two rows are identical, and no two columns are identical.

Binario+, the version in our puzzle books, adds signs between some squares. They are explained after the techniques.

Three Binairo solving techniques

1. Look for pairs and gaps

If two circles of the same colour sit side by side, the squares on either side of them must be the other colour. Otherwise you would have three in a row.

The same applies to a gap. If you see a circle, a blank, then a circle of the same colour, the blank must be the other colour.

2. Count the black and white circles

Once a row or column has all the black or all the white circles it needs, every remaining blank must be the other colour. Count each line as you go.

3. Compare rows and columns

No two rows can be the same, and no two columns can be the same. When a line is nearly finished, check whether one choice would make it a copy of another finished line. If so, use the other choice. You will see this in action in step 6 of the example below.

Binario+ signs

Binario+ is the version in our books. It adds a sign on the line between some pairs of squares:

  • = means the two squares hold the same colour.
  • × means they hold different colours.

All the rules and techniques above still apply. A sign next to a filled square settles its neighbour straight away, and that square can settle the next one.

Worked example: solving a 4×4 Binairo step by step

Follow along with a pencil. Circles drawn in orange are the ones we have filled in, and the highlighted squares are the ones we have just filled. Rows are numbered 1 to 4 from the top, and columns 1 to 4 from the left.

  1. Here is the puzzle. The circles drawn in ink are given, and we fill in the rest.

  2. Count. Row 3 already has two black circles. A row of four needs exactly two of each, so the other two squares in row 3 are white.

  3. Count. Column 3 now reads white, black, white and a blank. It needs two of each, so the blank in row 4 is black.

  4. Pair, then count. Row 4 ends in two black circles. That is a pair, so the square next to it can't hold a third black circle: it is white. Row 4 now has its two black circles, so the last blank is white as well.

  5. Count. Column 1 reads black, blank, black, white. It already has two black circles, so the blank in row 2 is white.

  6. Compare rows. Row 1 reads black, blank, white, blank, and needs one more of each colour. If column 2 were black, row 1 would read black, black, white, white, the same as row 3. That is not allowed, so column 2 is white and column 4 is black.

  7. Finish. Column 2 reads white, blank, black, white, so the blank in row 2 is black. Row 2 then needs one more white circle, which goes in column 4. The puzzle is solved.

Common Binairo mistakes

  • Checking rows but forgetting columns. Every rule applies to both. After placing a circle, glance along its column too.
  • Missing a gap. A pattern like black, blank, black is easy to overlook. Scan each line for it.
  • Miscounting. Write a small tally beside each row and column until you are confident.
  • Guessing too early. If you are stuck, look for pairs, gaps and counts you have not used yet. A good puzzle never needs a guess.
  • Forgetting the identical-lines rule. It matters most near the end, when one line is almost finished.
  • Overlooking a sign. In Binario+, check the signs around every square you fill. Each one may settle a neighbour.

Tip: solve in pencil and keep an eraser handy.

Binairo questions and answers

Is Binairo the same as Takuzu?

Yes. Binairo, Takuzu and Binary Puzzle are the same puzzle under different names, and the rules are identical. Some sites spell it Binario, and some print 0s and 1s instead of circles.

What is Binario+?

Binario+ is Binairo with = and × signs between some squares. It is the version in our books. The signs give you extra clues, so a Binario+ grid can start with fewer circles.

Do I need to be good at maths?

No. Binairo is about spotting patterns, not doing arithmetic.

What size should a beginner start with?

A 4×4 grid is good for learning the rules, and a 6×6 grid is a gentle next step. Move to larger grids when the techniques feel automatic.

Does every Binairo puzzle have only one solution?

A well-made puzzle does. The puzzles in this guide were checked by computer to confirm there is exactly one solution.

Can I guess if I get stuck?

You should not need to. Look again for pairs, gaps and counts, and check whether any line would become a copy of another.
