How to Solve Minesweeper Puzzles on Paper: Rules and Tips for Beginners

Updated 1 November 2026

Paper Minesweeper is the classic computer game turned into a calm logic puzzle. Some squares show a number, which counts the mines hidden in the eight squares around it. Your goal is to mark every hidden square as either a mine or safe. Unlike the computer game, there is no clicking and no luck: everything can be worked out from the numbers.

In short. Start with the zeros, whose neighbours are all safe. Then look for numbers that have exactly as many hidden neighbours as their value, and use squares you have already worked out to settle the rest.

What is paper Minesweeper?

If you have played Minesweeper on a computer, you already know the idea: a number tells you how many mines are next to it. On paper, all the numbers you will get are printed from the start, and there is no total mine count. You solve the whole grid with pencil logic, marking mines and safe squares as you go.

The numbered squares themselves are never mines. Every hidden square is either a mine or safe, and a well-made puzzle has exactly one way to decide them all.

Paper Minesweeper rules

  1. A number shows how many mines are in the up to eight squares touching it, including diagonals.
  2. Squares with numbers are never mines.
  3. Mark every hidden square as a mine or as safe, so that every number is correct.
  4. There is no total mine count: work only from the numbers shown.

Three Minesweeper solving techniques

1. Zeros make everything around them safe

A 0 means none of its neighbours is a mine. Mark all of its hidden neighbours as safe.

2. When the number equals the hidden squares, they are all mines

Count a number's hidden neighbours. If there are exactly as many as the number says, every one of them is a mine.

3. Use what you already know

Once some neighbours are marked, subtract them. If a 2 already touches two mines, its other hidden neighbours are safe. Numbers side by side can also settle each other, as in the classic 1-2-1 pattern below.

Worked example: solving a 5×5 Minesweeper step by step

Follow along with a pencil. Rows are numbered 1 to 5 from the top, and columns 1 to 5 from the left.

  1. Here is the puzzle. Nine squares are hidden, and each one is either a mine or safe.

  2. Zeros. The 0 at the start of row 3 touches two hidden squares, above and below it. Both are safe.

  3. Count the hidden squares. The 2 at the end of row 2 has just two hidden neighbours, in row 1, columns 4 and 5. Both are mines.

  4. Use what you know. The 1 in row 2, column 3 already touches the mine in row 1, column 4. So its other hidden neighbour, row 1, column 3, is safe.

  5. Only one place left. The 1 in row 2, column 2 needs one mine. Of its hidden neighbours, row 2, column 1 and row 1, column 3 are both safe. So the mine must be in row 1, column 1.

  6. Same idea, bottom left. The 1 in row 4, column 2 touches two hidden squares: row 4, column 1, which is safe, and row 5, column 1. That one must be the mine.

  7. Finish. The 2 in row 4, column 4 has exactly two hidden neighbours left, so both are mines. Every number is now satisfied.

Common Minesweeper mistakes

  • Forgetting the diagonals. A number counts all eight squares around it, including the corners.
  • Counting numbered squares as hidden. Only blank squares can be mines. Numbers never are.
  • Not marking safe squares. A slash for "safe" is as useful as a dot for "mine". It tells the next number what's left.
  • Guessing. There is no luck on paper. If you're stuck, look for two numbers side by side and compare what they share.

Minesweeper questions and answers

How is this different from the computer game?

The computer game reveals squares as you click and sometimes forces a guess. On paper, all the clues are there from the start and the puzzle is designed so you never need to guess.

Why is there no mine count?

These puzzles are designed to be solved from the numbers alone. Leaving out the total keeps every step local and logical.

What size should a beginner start with?

A 5×5 or 6×6 grid is ideal for learning. Bigger grids take longer but use the same few techniques.

Does every puzzle have only one solution?

A well-made puzzle does. The puzzles in this guide were checked by computer to confirm there is exactly one solution.
